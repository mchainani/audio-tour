Before you choose something to eat, look up. The iron columns, beams, and roof structure are your first clues. This is a market from the age when Buenos Aires was growing at extraordinary speed, and the practical business of feeding people called for ambitious architecture.

The Mercado de San Telmo opened in 1897. Its architect, Juan Antonio Buschiazzo, had come from Italy as a child and became an important figure in the city's development. His market combined a street façade with the large, sheltered interior needed for everyday commerce. Metal and glass helped bring light into the building while allowing a broad space for stalls and circulation. The structure survived as what people bought inside it changed.

Try a small exercise. Find one old structural element and one very contemporary business. Hold them in the same view. That combination is San Telmo: inherited buildings adapted by people who need to make a living now.

By the time the market opened, immigration was transforming Buenos Aires. Large numbers of Italians and Spaniards arrived, alongside people from many other places. Some came with skills, relatives, or a little capital. Others arrived with almost nothing. A port could promise a new life without making that life easy.

Many newcomers rented rooms in conventillos, shared tenement houses arranged around courtyards. A building that had once served one prosperous household might now accommodate many families, sharing taps, washing areas, and toilets. Those patios could be spaces of friendship, music, and mutual help. They could also mean overcrowding, disputes, and poor sanitation. The picturesque courtyard and the difficult living conditions belong to the same history.

You will often hear that wealthy residents abandoned the southern neighborhoods after the yellow fever epidemic of 1871 and moved north. The epidemic was devastating, and it accelerated residential changes. But the shift was a longer process, involving wealth, transport, land development, and public health. One dramatic year cannot explain the whole geography of the city.

Nor did immigration create culture on an empty stage. Afro-Argentines, Indigenous people, and people arriving from other parts of the country were already part of Buenos Aires. African-descended communities helped shape its work, festivities, and music. Later narratives that imagined Argentina as exclusively European pushed those contributions out of view. A good market is a useful correction: actual daily life is always more mixed than a national slogan.

Now, food. You will see international cooking here as well as Argentine favorites. A market changes with its customers, so there is no need to treat every modern counter as an ancient tradition. Instead, let us learn a few things you can recognize across the city.

An empanada is a filled pastry, but saying that is a little like calling wine a drink. Regional differences matter. Fillings may include beef, chicken, cheese, vegetables, or humita, a corn preparation. Dough can be baked or fried. Some beef fillings are finely chopped, others minced. Cumin, olives, egg, potato, and the level of juiciness depend on region and recipe. The folded edge, called the repulgue, can help identify a filling within a particular shop, although there is no universal code obeyed by every cook.

If someone offers you a choripán, the name gives you the recipe's outline: chorizo and pan, sausage and bread. A grilled sausage is split or placed whole in a bread roll, often accompanied by chimichurri or salsa criolla. Chimichurri commonly brings together herbs, garlic, oil, vinegar, and seasoning. Salsa criolla is usually a chopped mixture including onion, pepper, and tomato. Every cook has opinions. This is an excellent country in which to discover that a condiment can be a serious subject.

A parrilla is a grill and also a restaurant organized around grilling. An asado can mean cuts of meat, a style of cooking, or the social occasion itself. Its deepest cultural meaning is often about time: someone tending the fire, people arriving, conversation continuing, food emerging in stages. Argentina's cattle economy helped make beef central to national identity, but Argentine eating is more varied than a single grill. Vegetarian meals, regional stews, Indigenous ingredients, and contemporary kitchens are part of the picture too.

The Italian influence is especially easy to taste. Think of pizza, fresh pasta, and helado, Argentine ice cream. A milanesa, a breaded cutlet, belongs to this conversation as well. In Buenos Aires, imported dishes developed local habits and meanings. The result is not simply Europe served overseas. It is food shaped by migration, local ingredients, prices, and generations of family cooking.

For something sweet, learn two words: dulce de leche. Milk and sugar are cooked slowly until the mixture becomes thick and caramel-colored. It appears in pastries, ice cream, cakes, and alfajores, the filled sandwich confections sold in countless styles. Several countries have their own histories and names for related sweets. You do not need to settle a national ownership dispute before enjoying one.

Coffee has its own vocabulary. A café is generally a small coffee; a cortado has a little milk; a café con leche has more. A medialuna is a small crescent-shaped pastry, often softer and sweeter than the French croissant you may have in mind. Exact preparations vary, so point and ask. The useful phrase is, “¿Qué me recomendás?” What do you recommend? That ending, recomendás, belongs to the local use of vos, which we will hear more about later.

And there is mate, pronounced in two syllables. It is an infusion of yerba mate, traditionally drunk through a filtered metal straw called a bombilla. Its roots lie in Indigenous South America, especially Guaraní traditions, and it is shared across several countries. In a mate circle, the person preparing it, the cebador, usually pours and passes each serving. If you are invited, follow their lead and leave the straw in place. Saying gracias commonly signals that you are finished, rather than asking for another round. Preferences vary, and sharing is always optional. The larger lesson is hospitality: a simple drink can create a small circle of belonging.

One distinction before you wander. This indoor market is not the same thing as the Sunday antiques fair around Plaza Dorrego or the outdoor stalls along Defensa. They are nearby experiences with different histories and rhythms. You do not have to arrive on a Sunday to understand the market building.

Let yourself choose one thing slowly. Ask what it is, where it is made, or how it is usually eaten. That short exchange tells you something an architecture label cannot. From here, our itinerary continues south to La Boca, where migration and neighborhood pride find a very loud expression in football.
