Bienvenidos a Buenos Aires. Welcome. Before we begin, find a comfortable place in the square and take in the buildings around you. The pink palace. The white colonial arches. The cathedral that looks, rather surprisingly, like a Greek temple. We could introduce Argentina through any one of them. But the most important part of this place is the space between the buildings: the ground where people gather.

This is Plaza de Mayo. Governments have tried to speak from its balconies. Crowds have answered from its paving stones. Sometimes they have celebrated together. Sometimes the conversation has been a confrontation. As we explore the city, keep this square in your mind. Many of the stories we meet will lead us back here.

First, a little geography. Buenos Aires is a port city on the Río de la Plata, the immense estuary shared with Uruguay. A person from the city is a porteño or porteña: someone of the port. That identity matters. Ships, goods, migrants, music, and ideas have entered Argentina through this waterfront. But the port's wealth has also given Buenos Aires a complicated relationship with the rest of the country. The capital is influential; it is never the whole of Argentina.

And Argentina's history does not begin with a European ship. Indigenous peoples lived across this region long before Spanish colonization. The Querandí were among the peoples encountered around the first Spanish settlement. Their lives and territories were disrupted by invasion, disease, and conflict. When you hear someone describe an empty land waiting to become a city, remember that it was not empty.

The Spanish attempted a settlement under Pedro de Mendoza in 1536. Hunger and conflict helped doom it. The lasting colonial foundation came in 1580, under Juan de Garay, who laid out a settlement around this area. That is why Buenos Aires is often said to have been founded twice. The phrase is useful, provided we remember whose act of founding it describes.

For much of the colonial period, this was a relatively peripheral city in Spain's American empire. Trade restrictions favored other routes, while merchants here wanted access to Atlantic markets. Legal commerce and contraband both helped shape the port. In 1776, Buenos Aires became the capital of the new Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata. Its political importance grew considerably.

Now look toward the white building with an arcade and a central tower, across the square from the Casa Rosada. That is the Cabildo, the colonial town council. Its present appearance is the result of rebuilding and restoration; it once stretched farther across the frontage. You are looking at a surviving institution and site, rather than an untouched photograph of the eighteenth century.

In 1806 and 1807, British forces tried to take Buenos Aires. Local resistance defeated them. The invasions demonstrated that the city could organize armed resistance without depending entirely on Spain. Then Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 threw the monarchy into crisis. If the king had been displaced, where did legitimate authority now reside? For people on this side of the Atlantic, that was an explosive question.

During the May Revolution of 1810, the Cabildo became a central stage in the struggle over that authority. On May twenty-fifth, the viceroy was replaced by a local governing junta. That is the May commemorated in the square's name.

It is easy to compress this into one triumphant sentence: Argentina became independent here. The actual story took longer. The revolution opened a process of war, political experimentation, and conflict across a much larger region. Formal independence was declared in Tucumán on July ninth, 1816. And the creation of a unified Argentine state involved many more decades of struggle. May twenty-fifth is a beginning, not the ending of the story.

Near the center of the plaza stands the Pirámide de Mayo, a pale monument crowned by a female figure representing liberty. Its first version was erected in 1811, on the revolution's first anniversary, and it was later transformed. Even a monument to a single event accumulates layers.

So does the plaza itself. The open space you see today was once divided by a building called the Recova, an arcaded commercial structure. Its demolition in 1884 helped create the unified square. Imagine trying to assemble a crowd here when shops and masonry cut across the middle. The present setting for national demonstrations was partly made through nineteenth-century urban planning.

Now turn to the Casa Rosada, the Pink House. It is the seat of the national executive and the president's workplace. Its site once guarded the city as a colonial fort, close to the old waterfront. The river's edge was much nearer than the modern cityscape suggests. Successive buildings were altered and joined into the palace we recognize today.

People love to explain its color. One story says that pink reconciled the red associated with the Federalists and the white associated with the Unitarians, rival political traditions in nineteenth-century Argentina. Another connects it to old limewash mixtures using animal blood. Both stories circulate widely. Neither should be treated as a neatly proven explanation of every pink wall. Sometimes a good guide's job is to leave a familiar story with its question mark attached.

The balconies are closely associated with Juan Domingo Perón and Eva Perón. On October seventeenth, 1945, a vast workers' mobilization demanded Perón's release from detention. The date became foundational to Peronism, the political movement that continues to shape Argentine life.

For many workers, Peronism meant labor rights, social benefits, and recognition from a state that had often seemed distant. For opponents, it also represented concentrated power and pressure on independent institutions. Those arguments are still alive. You will understand Argentina better if you resist turning a movement spanning generations into either a love song or a villain's portrait.

Eva, usually called Evita, became one of its most powerful voices. Her story will return at Recoleta Cemetery. For now, look at the distance between a balcony and the ground. That connection between a leader above and a crowd below is one of the defining political images of this country.

This square has also witnessed terrible violence. On June sixteenth, 1955, naval aircraft and other forces taking part in an attempted coup bombed the area around the government palace, killing hundreds of people. The political heart of the city became a site of mass civilian death. A few months later, a military coup overthrew Perón. The relationship between democracy, armed forces, and political exclusion remained deeply troubled.

The next part of our story concerns state terror and missing people. During the last dictatorship, from 1976 to 1983, the armed forces and security services abducted, tortured, and murdered people through a clandestine system of repression. The disappeared are known as the desaparecidos. Many families were denied even the basic knowledge of what had happened to someone they loved.

In 1977, mothers searching for their children began gathering here. The first meeting was on April thirtieth. When police ordered them to keep moving, walking together became a form of protest. Their white headscarves became an internationally recognized symbol. Look for representations of those scarves around the pyramid, if they are visible during your visit.

The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo made an intensely personal demand into a public challenge: tell us where our children are. The Grandmothers of Plaza de Mayo developed a related search for children taken or born in captivity and raised under other identities. Their work has included the use of genetics to restore identities. These histories involve distinct organizations and, over time, different political positions. What connects them is the insistence that a state cannot simply erase a person.

Democratic government returned in 1983. Argentina's subsequent trials, memory work, and debates about justice have been difficult and consequential. This is living history, not a closed exhibition. If you encounter a vigil or demonstration here, give the participants space. For someone beside you, a name on a banner may belong to a parent, a sibling, or a friend.

Before we leave, notice the cathedral's classical columns. Behind that frontage is a Catholic church containing the mausoleum of José de San Martín, whose military campaigns helped secure independence in Argentina, Chile, and Peru. Jorge Mario Bergoglio served here as archbishop before becoming Pope Francis in 2013. The building brings religious life, national memory, and international history into the same corner of the square.

Take one last look across the plaza. Colonial council, presidential palace, cathedral, monument, and people. Buenos Aires is often introduced through its elegant façades. Here, the deeper introduction is a question: who has the right to speak for the country? You will hear different answers as we travel.
